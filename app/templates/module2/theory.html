Part D — The two-camera relationship

Derivation of the relationship between the image coordinates of a point P = (X, Y, Z) in camera 1 and in camera 2, where camera 1 is static and camera 2 sits at some distance and at an oblique orientation from it, with P inside the field of view of both. Typed, as the submission requires; the same text is in theory/two_camera_derivation.md for the PDF.

1. Assumptions, and why each is defensible

  1. Ideal pinhole projection. Both images are undistorted first using the coefficients from Step 1. With RMS reprojection error below 0.5 px after correction, the residual departure from a pinhole is below the corner-localization noise, so modelling it further adds parameters without reducing error.
  2. Both cameras calibrated. K1 and K2 known from Step 1. If one phone captures both views then K1 = K2, which halves the unknown intrinsics.
  3. Zero skew, square pixels. Step 1 gives fy/fx within a fraction of a percent of 1, and CMOS pixel axes are orthogonal by construction, so the skew term is set to zero rather than estimated.
  4. Rigid relative pose. R and t are constant across the pair of exposures — the rig does not flex between them.
  5. World frame anchored to camera 1. A free choice of gauge, and the one that makes the algebra clean: camera 1's extrinsics become [I | 0].
  6. P in front of both cameras — positive depth in both frames, the chirality condition, implied by "within the FoV of both".
  7. Static scene. P does not move between exposures; required when the two views are captured sequentially with one phone.

2. Setting up the two projections

With x ≅ K[R|t]X and the world frame on camera 1:

Camera 1: x₁ ≅ K₁ [I | 0] X = K₁ P Camera 2: x₂ ≅ K₂ [R | t] X = K₂ (R P + t)

P = (X, Y, Z)T is nonhomogeneous, in camera 1's frame; R is camera 2's rotation relative to camera 1; and t = −R C̃ with C̃ the camera 2 centre expressed in camera 1's frame. "Oblique orientation" means R ≠ I; "at some distance" means t ≠ 0. Both matter below.

3. Back out P from camera 1

P = Z · K₁⁻¹ x₁

Exactly the backprojection used in Step 2 — the same operation, reused. It also makes the difficulty explicit: x1 determines only the ray on which P lies. The scalar Z is the missing information.

4. Substitute — the answer

x₂ ≅ K₂ R (Z K₁⁻¹ x₁) + K₂ t = Z (K₂ R K₁⁻¹) x₁ + K₂ t

Dividing through by Z, legal because the relation is projective:

x₂ ≅ H∞ x₁ + e₂ / Z where H∞ = K₂ R K₁⁻¹ the infinite homography e₂ = K₂ t the epipole in image 2

This is the required relationship. In words: the image of P in camera 2 is the position of x1 warped by rotation and by the two sets of intrinsics, then displaced along the direction of the epipole e2 by an amount inversely proportional to depth.

  • H∞ is the limit as Z → ∞. Points at infinity map by H∞ alone, which is why distant scenery barely shifts between two views while nearby objects shift a lot.
  • Sweeping Z from 0 to ∞ traces the epipolar line of x1 in image 2. A single view therefore cannot recover depth — the whole line is consistent with one observation. This is exactly why Step 2 needed a tape measure.

5. The depth-free form: the epipolar constraint

Since P₂ = RP + t makes P2, t and RP coplanar, the triple product vanishes:

P₂ · (t × R P) = 0

In normalized coordinates, rearranged into matrix form:

x₂ᵀ F x₁ = 0 where F = K₂⁻ᵀ [t]ₓ R K₁⁻¹ the fundamental matrix E = [t]ₓ R = K₂ᵀ F K₁ the essential matrix
quantityDoFwhy
F7 3×3, defined up to scale (−1), and det F = 0 (−1)
E5 3 for R, 3 for t, minus 1 for the unrecoverable scale of t

Because E is unchanged by scaling t, translation is recoverable only up to scale from correspondences alone. Metric scale needs one real length: a checkerboard square, or a tape-measured baseline ‖t‖.

6. Expanded scalar form

With the rows of R written r1T, r2T, r3T:

u₂ = f_x2 · (r₁ᵀP + t_x) / (r₃ᵀP + t_z) + c_x2 v₂ = f_y2 · (r₂ᵀP + t_y) / (r₃ᵀP + t_z) + c_y2

The denominator r3TP + tz is the depth of P in camera 2's frame. This form makes concrete what the matrix form hides: the mapping is nonlinear in P because of the perspective division, and the nonlinearity sits entirely in that one shared denominator. Parametrizing R by ZYZ Euler angles, R(ψ,θ,φ) = R_z(φ) R_y(θ) R_z(ψ), makes the complete set of static unknowns (ψ, θ, φ, tx, ty, tz) — six numbers, five recoverable from correspondences, the sixth needing one external length.

8. Degenerate and special cases

Pure rotation, t = 0

x₂ ≅ H∞ x₁ (exactly, with no depth term)

Every point maps by one homography regardless of distance, so depth is unrecoverable and F is undefined (E = [0]×R = 0). This is the sharpest argument that a nonzero baseline is essential: panning a phone on a tripod yields no depth information no matter how many frames are taken.

P on a plane

If all points satisfy nTP = d, then Z can be eliminated using the plane equation and the mapping becomes a plane-induced homography H = K₂(R + t nᵀ/d)K₁⁻¹, again independent of per-point depth. This is the same coplanar-point degeneracy that makes a single checkerboard view insufficient for calibration — and why Zhang's method needs the board in several orientations.

P on the baseline

P projects to the epipole in both images and x2TFx1 = 0 holds identically. The correspondence carries no information and triangulation is singular.

9. Inverting the derivation: triangulation

The payoff is that the two-camera formulation removes Step 2's dependence on a manually measured Z. With x1, x2, R, t and both intrinsics known, writing each projection as a cross product x × (P X) = 0 yields a homogeneous linear system A X = 0, solved by the singular vector of A with the smallest singular value — which is what cv2.triangulatePoints does. Real-world dimensions then follow as the Euclidean distance between two triangulated points, with no tape measure anywhere in the pipeline.

That directly resolves the main limitation of the experimental part: Step 2's accuracy is bounded below by how well Z can be measured by hand, and Step 3's statistics show the resulting bias explicitly. The cost is that metric scale must still enter once, via the baseline or a known length in the scene — depth from two views is otherwise determined only up to a single global scale factor.
