\section{Step 1: Camera calibration}

A smartphone camera was calibrated with OpenCV's built-in tools by
\href{https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/2016/02/tr98-71.pdf}%
{Zhang's multi-plane method}. A $9 \times 6$ inner-corner checkerboard with a measured
square size of $24.9\mm$ was photographed 24 times in varied orientations.
Corners were located and refined to sub-pixel accuracy, and the camera model
was then fitted with \texttt{cv2.calibrateCamera}, which minimises total
reprojection error over the intrinsics, the distortion coefficients and the
per-view pose. Focus, exposure and zoom were locked for the whole set, and
every image was captured at the one resolution later used in Steps 2 and 3.

The recovered camera matrix and distortion coefficients are

\begin{equation}

\begin{bmatrix}

0 & 3335.58 & 2020.27 \\
0 & 0 & 1
\end{bmatrix},
\qquad
\mathbf{d} =
\begin{bmatrix}
0.2231 \\ -1.3758 \\ 0.00058 \\ -0.00081 \\ 2.7090
\end{bmatrix},
\end{equation}

\noindent
in the ordering $(k_1, k_2, p_1, p_2, k_3)$. Since $f$ and $c$ are in pixels,

\section{Step 2: Real-world dimensions from perspective projection}

Under the pinhole model a point $P = (X, Y, Z)$ in camera coordinates projects
to

\begin{equation}
u = f_x \frac{X}{Z} + c_x, \qquad v = f_y \frac{Y}{Z} + c_y .
\label{eq:forward}
\end{equation}

A single image cannot recover $P$, since every point on the ray through $(u,v)$
maps to the same pixel. Supplying the object distance $Z$ independently removes
that one unknown and makes Equation~\ref{eq:forward} invertible:

\begin{equation}
X = (u - c_x)\frac{Z}{f_x}, \qquad Y = (v - c_y)\frac{Z}{f_y} .
\label{eq:backproject}
\end{equation}

A dimension between two selected points $p_1$ and $p_2$ lying on a common plane
at depth $Z$ is then the Euclidean distance between their backprojections,

\begin{equation}
L = \lVert P_2 - P_1 \rVert
  = Z \sqrt{ \left(\frac{\Delta u}{f_x}\right)^{\!2}
           + \left(\frac{\Delta v}{f_y}\right)^{\!2} } ,
\label{eq:length}
\end{equation}

\noindent
which for each axis comes out to:

\begin{equation}
W = \Delta u \frac{Z}{f_x}, \qquad H = \Delta v \frac{Z}{f_y} .
\label{eq:wh}
\end{equation}

Width, height and diagonal therefore all come from Equation~\ref{eq:length} by
one code path. Both endpoints must lie at the
same depth $Z$, and lens distortion is
removed from the two selected points only, with
\texttt{cv2.undistortPoints}, which returns \emph{normalised} coordinates,
already $X/Z$ and $Y/Z$, so the dimension is
$Z \cdot \lVert \Delta(\text{normalised}) \rVert$ with no further division by
$f_x$.

Figure~\ref{fig:step2} Shows an example. The two selected
points span $1069.7\px$ across the top edge of a laptop lid at a tape-measured
$Z = 1000\mm$, with a checkerboard displayed on its screen, and
Equation~\ref{eq:length} turns that span into $317.7\mm$ against a ruler truth
of $356.0\mm$. It is entry 1 of the validation set in Step 3.

\section{Theory: relating the image of a point in two cameras}

Camera 1 is static; camera 2 sits at some distance from it and at an oblique
orientation. A point $P = (X, Y, Z)$ lies in the field of view of both. What
follows relates $x_1$, the image of $P$ in camera 1, to $x_2$, its image in
camera 2. Once the assumptions below are in place, the relationship is one
substitution.

\subsection{Assumptions}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Ideal pinhole projection.} Both images are undistorted first,
        using the distortion coefficients from Step 1. After correction the
        residual departure from an ideal pinhole is below the
        corner-localisation noise, so modelling it further would add
        parameters without reducing error.
  \item \textbf{Both cameras are calibrated.} $K_1$ and $K_2$ are known from a
        Zhang calibration as in Step 1. If one phone captures both views, as
        it does here, then $K_1 = K_2 = K$ and half the unknown intrinsics
        disappear.
  \item \textbf{Zero skew, square pixels.} The Step 1 fit gives $f_y/f_x$
        within $0.03\%$ of unity and CMOS pixel axes are orthogonal by
        construction, so the skew term in $K$ is set to zero rather than
        estimated.
  \item \textbf{Rigid relative pose.} $R$ and $\mathbf{t}$ are constant for the
        pair of exposures. With one phone moved by hand they describe those two
        exposures and nothing else.
  \item \textbf{World frame anchored to camera 1.} Camera 1's
        extrinsics become $[\,I \mid \mathbf{0}\,]$, so $P$ in the world frame
        is also $P$ in camera 1's frame.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{The derivation}

With the world frame on camera 1, the two projections are

\begin{equation}
x_1 = K_1 P,
\qquad
x_2 = K_2 (R P + \mathbf{t}),
\label{eq:twoproj}
\end{equation}

\noindent
where $x_1 = (u_1, v_1, 1)^{\!\top}$ and $x_2 = (u_2, v_2, 1)^{\!\top}$ are the
image points, $R$ is camera 2's rotation relative to camera 1, a proper 3D
rotation with $R^{-1} = R^{\!\top}$, and $\mathbf{t} = -R\tilde{C}$ with
$\tilde{C}$ the centre of camera
2 expressed in camera 1's frame. ``Oblique orientation'' is the statement
$R \neq I$, and ``at some distance'' is $\mathbf{t} \neq \mathbf{0}$.

Only the first of the two has to be inverted, and Step 2 already did it. The
intrinsic matrix is upper triangular, so its inverse is immediate,

\begin{equation}
K_1 =
\begin{bmatrix}
f_{x1} & 0 & c_{x1} \\ 0 & f_{y1} & c_{y1} \\ 0 & 0 & 1
\end{bmatrix},
\qquad
K_1^{-1} =
\begin{bmatrix}
1/f_{x1} & 0 & -c_{x1}/f_{x1} \\
0 & 1/f_{y1} & -c_{y1}/f_{y1} \\
0 & 0 & 1
\end{bmatrix},
\label{eq:kinv}
\end{equation}

\noindent
and applying it to $x_1$ strips the intrinsics off the measured pixel:

\begin{equation}
K_1^{-1} x_1 =
\begin{pmatrix}
(u_1 - c_{x1})/f_{x1} \\ (v_1 - c_{y1})/f_{y1} \\ 1
\end{pmatrix}
=
\begin{pmatrix} X/Z \\ Y/Z \\ 1 \end{pmatrix},
\qquad\text{so}\qquad
P = Z\,K_1^{-1} x_1 .
\label{eq:invert}
\end{equation}

The middle step is Equation~\ref{eq:backproject} written as a vector: $x_1$
fixes the direction of the ray on which $P$ lies, and the scalar $Z$ says how
far along that ray $P$ sits. Substituting this $P$ into camera 2's projection
is then the whole derivation,

\begin{align}
x_2 &= K_2 (R P + \mathbf{t}) \notag \\
    &= K_2 \bigl( R \,(Z\,K_1^{-1} x_1) + \mathbf{t} \bigr)
       &&\text{(substitute $P$)} \notag \\
    &= Z\,K_2 R K_1^{-1} x_1 + K_2 \mathbf{t}
       &&\text{($R$, $K_2$ are linear; $Z$ is a scalar)} \notag \\
    &= Z \left( K_2 R K_1^{-1} x_1 + \frac{1}{Z}\,K_2 \mathbf{t} \right)
       &&\text{(factor out $Z$).}
\label{eq:chain}
\end{align}

That leading $Z$ multiplies every entry of the bracket, including the third, so
it divides straight back out when the pixels are read off and can be dropped.
What is left is the relationship between the two images of $P$:

\begin{equation}
\boxed{\;
x_2 \;=\; K_2 R K_1^{-1}\, x_1 \;+\; \frac{1}{Z}\,K_2 \mathbf{t} \;}
\label{eq:main}
\end{equation}

The image of $P$ in camera 2 is therefore $x_1$ warped by the rotation and the
two sets of intrinsics, then displaced by an amount inversely proportional to
the depth of $P$. That depth is the only per-point quantity in the relation;
everything else is fixed for the pair of cameras.
